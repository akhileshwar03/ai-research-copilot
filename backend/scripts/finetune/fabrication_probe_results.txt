A new study finds that corals off the coast of the Maldives are recovering twice as fast as scientists thought they would.

The paper from the University of Exeter's Global Systems Institute was published Monday in the Journal of Marine Biology, and comes with good news for those concerned about ocean health — especially given a recent report detailing how rising temperatures have left corals around the world at risk for extinction by 2050.

Researchers studied coral reefs near the Maldives over an 18-month period following a particularly strong warming event in 2022. They found that the coral was recovering about 23% faster than expected, according to an Exeter press release.

"We found that corals off the coast of the Maldives were actually more resilient than previously thought," lead author Matt Dunbabin said in the statement. "This is particularly important given the ongoing threat from climate change."

The Maldives has been a leader in sustainable tourism for decades, with some of its resorts being powered by solar power since 1983.

The Maldives are located in the Indian Ocean and have become well-known as an idyllic tourist destination known for luxury resorts. However, the country is one of many that could be hard hit by climate change. The islands sit just a few feet above sea level on average.

As temperatures rise around the world, coral reefs will face increasing danger from bleaching events. When water gets too warm — usually as a result of increased greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, or natural processes like volcanic activity — corals lose their color and can die off over time. The 2022 event was particularly severe.

Dunbabin said that the Maldives' reefs are especially vulnerable to bleaching because water is shallow in areas where coral grow, meaning there's less of a buffer from temperature swings.

"The fact that they're seeing such resilience is very promising," Dunbabin told The Independent. "If we can make corals more resilient, it can help them fight off the effects of climate change."

One particularly promising finding was that reefs with higher diversity were able to recover much faster than reefs made up mainly of a single type of coral.

"You don't have to worry about one species being knocked out and then there's nothing left," co-author Simon Davy said in the press release. "Diversity is your friend."

The next step for Dunbabin and his team will be looking at how other types of reefs around the world are doing, especially those in places like Australia where coral has been hit hard by recent bleaching events.

This year's bleaching event was likely caused primarily by climate change, according to a study published last month. And it wasn't just the Maldives that saw widespread damage; reefs across much of Southeast Asia were also affected by high temperatures this summer.

The paper notes that there are still many unknowns about how corals will respond over time to rising temperatures — and that these findings don't mean climate change isn't a threat anymore. But it does suggest that the future is slightly less bleak than some had feared.

"Even with increasing global warming, we're likely to see some of our coral reefs surviving if we make efforts to reduce emissions," Davy said in the release.
