Open-plan offices are a modern standard in corporate workplaces. The idea is laudable: Break down walls between cubicles or floors to foster collaboration and break apart the old power structure that kept higher-ups on pedestals from which they looked down on their subordinates—right? Wrong.

Despite all of open-plan's supposed benefits, research increasingly shows it actually makes people less productive while simultaneously doing nothing for teamwork at most companies.

The promise behind open-plan offices is simple: By getting rid of barriers to communication like cubicle walls or floors, employees will start chatting more easily and bouncing ideas off one another. This sounds noble enough when we think about the old corporate hierarchy, in which a small group sat at the top with all their information under wraps from everyone below them.

However, despite what popular design trends might have you believe, that top-down model doesn't exactly exist anymore in most workplaces—unless of course your organization is one whose leaders can only see things clearly through rose-tinted glasses. Open-plan offices create a culture where employees' attention span and productivity suffer under the weight of constant distraction.

Noise in open-plan office

You walk down the hallway, and every room seems filled with activity at any moment—heads bent over computers, phones ringing, people shuffling about between desks—and it becomes clear there's no place to go. Every employee you see is a potential interruption: their phone ringing in public or a colleague walking into an open cubicle for a short conversation.

This level of ambient chatter can be incredibly disruptive, breaking anyone out of the deep focus state required to solve truly tricky problems as well as creative work with any rigor and care necessary.

A 2018 study published in The Journal of Environmental Psychology found that employees who worked from open-office layouts expressed greater dissatisfaction than those working behind cubicles or closed offices. This is perhaps unsurprising, but what might surprise you are the reasons they cited: noise, lack of privacy, not a shortage in office supplies.

And no amount of whiteboards and brainstorming sessions will make up for these shortcomings. Employees reported needing to wear headphones at work to tune out constant distractions. Others said that they simply retreated into "quiet corners" or coffee shops to do their work because the interruptions were too much, and even then couldn't seem to concentrate.

The assumptions behind open offices

When a new trend like this catches on quickly, it's usually only an overzealous version of what really works. However, for those who've had bad experiences with open-plan design but think that might have just been a coincidence in layout or management styles, read the fine print before you believe.

The thinking behind these office designs is flawed and ultimately misaligned not because it can't work, but rather due to its insistence on getting people to do more of their productive work together than apart. The assumption here—that physical proximity equals greater collaboration—isn't necessarily true when applied in isolation as a one-size-fits-all model.

The point that this design misses is simple: When collaboration works well it's usually purposeful and done at the right times—like during regular standups, or after work when people meet up for drinks. By putting everyone all together constantly you create an illusion of teamwork where none exists. As a result, employees are interrupted out of deep focus while trying to get their most difficult tasks completed.

There's no denying that we live in collaborative spaces like this now and can't just go back to silos of cubicles separated by walls—though given the number of organizations who have embraced these layouts, it seems unlikely. That being said, there are thoughtful ways designers could improve open-plan offices so they foster teamwork rather than stifle productivity.

Creating a flexible environment

The better way lies in acknowledging that people aren't exactly the same when working on specific tasks; some employees will perform best with deep focus and quiet settings for long periods while others may need to chat and bounce ideas around throughout their days. What open-plan offices usually fail at is offering enough choices between collaborative, more productive spaces as well as private ones.

Employees should be encouraged to work in whichever space suits them most given the task or moment—like using a corner of desks in an office with no walls rather than a formal conference room when they need to discuss something quickly.
