What is wrong about open plan offices?

Open plan offices were invented out of what seemed like a good idea. By removing physical barriers between people, the thinking goes, employees will communicate more spontaneously and share ideas with each other more freely. The result will be increased collaboration.

Unfortunately the reality has been anything but rosy. The office chatter may seem lively, but the truth is it's far from conducive to getting any work done. A constant hum of noise and visual distractions – phones ringing or people chatting at their desks, not to mention the sight of your colleagues bustling about on a mission of their own – breaks you out of concentration mode.

Not that anyone really needs studies confirming this intuitive experience; in fact numerous investigations have confirmed what should be obvious: workers in open plan offices are less productive and suffer from higher stress levels than those who work in private or semi-private spaces. The 2018 study published in the Journal of Environmental Psychology found noise, lack of privacy to be most detrimental factors.

What's more, the assumption that being physically closer is good for working together isn't borne out by evidence either. It all depends on what you're trying to achieve – and collaboration tends to be far from spontaneous when it comes to getting important work done. In many cases employees need uninterrupted individual focus followed by meetings or brainstorming sessions. Open plan offices blur the boundaries between these different modes of working, leading to a disjointed workday that doesn't do justice to either.

So what's better? Thoughtfully designed offices include a mix of private spaces and quiet zones as well as collaborative areas – this way employees can choose when they want to connect with colleagues rather than having collaboration forced on them. This is much more likely to lead to productive teamwork.

In the end, open plan offices were invented out of what seemed like a good idea. But in practice they tend to do anything but connect people; indeed all too often they saps productivity through constant distractions and reduces the quality of teamwork they're supposed to facilitate. It's time employers started thinking outside the box – or better still without it.
